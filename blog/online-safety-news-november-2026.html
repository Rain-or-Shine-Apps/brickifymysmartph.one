Online safety news: edtech, nude filters and chatbots

12 November 2026 · By Anne Mundy

← Back to Parenting & phones

Three stories caught my eye this month, and a fourth that's still only reported. Here's what each one means for parents.

1. The classroom has become a screen

A new report from Digital Futures for Children (a joint project of the LSE and 5Rights Foundation) talked to 454 children aged 6 to 18 in 11 schools across all four UK nations about the tech they use in lessons. What they described would worry most parents:

  • Speed over understanding. Children lost points for right answers that came too slowly. Timers and leaderboards made learning about "collecting points, climbing leaderboards and competing with others", in the words of one 17-year-old.
  • Adverts in lessons. A YouTube advert for Uber Eats interrupted a lesson on the Holocaust, and a younger child saw an advert featuring nudity.
  • Behaviour points and tracking. Systems logged good and bad behaviour points, and in some schools even trips to the toilet. Children said they felt watched "all the time".
  • Homework nagging at midnight. Automated reminders chased work that was already handed in, and one child was woken at midnight.

The week before, an MP told the Commons science and technology committee that Times Tables Rock Stars rewards children for how long they spend on it. The schools minister, Paul Waugh, replied: "Gamification isn't the way to learn." The company behind it says it rewards progress, not time, and that from 24 November a usage curfew and the leaderboard switch-off become the default settings.

Many schools are rightly going smartphone-free, but much of the screen time has simply moved onto school devices and homework apps, and parents get very little say over those.

Sources: 5Rights Foundation, Schools Week

2. Apple and Google were given three months. They didn't make it

On 8 June the government gave Apple and Google three months to switch on protections that stop children taking, sharing or viewing nude images on their phones. When the deadline passed in September, the government said both firms had made progress, but not enough to "meet the scale" of the problem. It promised to legislate instead. Under its plans:

  • no one under 18 could take, view or send a nude image;
  • blocking would cover the whole device, including the camera and other companies' apps, not just the built-in messaging app;
  • the filters would be on by default.

That bill hasn't been put before Parliament yet. MPs and peers want to go further. Led by Jess Phillips, who resigned as safeguarding minister in May over the slow progress, and Lord Nash in the Lords, they're calling for every phone and tablet sold in the UK to block child sexual abuse material, on adult phones as well as children's.

For now, what's already on the phone helps, but only up to a point. Apple's Communication Safety and Google's sensitive content warnings blur nudity that's detected on a child's account, but they don't cover every app. It's worth checking they're switched on in Screen Time or Family Link.

Sources: gov.uk, BBC News

3. A law to stop chatbots harming children

Adam Raine was 16. He started using ChatGPT for help with geometry and Spanish homework. His parents say the chatbot became his closest confidant, discouraged him from turning to the adults around him when he began to struggle, and did nothing to stop him. He died in April 2025.

His mother, Maria Raine, has campaigned alongside Common Sense Media ever since. On 28 October New York's governor signed a law that bars AI chatbots, ChatGPT and Gemini as well as companion apps such as Character.ai and Replika, from offering under-18s features that encourage self-harm, suicide or eating disorders, pretend to be human or play a companion, or put engagement ahead of safety. Common Sense calls it the strongest children's AI safety law in the US, and is now campaigning to bring it to other states.

Nothing like it exists in the UK yet. Many chatbots are built into apps children already use, and the ones that sell themselves as homework help are the easiest to say yes to.

Sources: New York Governor's office, Common Sense Media

If this story has raised anything for you or your child, Samaritans are available any time on 116 123, and children can call Childline free on 0800 1111.

4. The under-16 ban could reach YouTube Kids and Spotify

The UK's ban on social media for under-16s is due to start in March 2027. This week the Mirror reported that it could cover up to 150 services, including YouTube Kids, Spotify, Lego Play, gaming platforms, and marketplaces such as eBay and Vinted. Nothing is confirmed yet. Lisa Nandy, the Culture Secretary, is expected to set out the full scope in December. If the reports are right, many more of the apps on a child's phone will need age checks, or will need to show they're safe for under-16s.

Source: The Mirror, via AOL

Where Brickify fits into all of this

Brickify turns a smartphone into a brick phone on your own schedule. It won't solve everything above, so here's what it can and can't do:

  1. It doesn't control school devices. Edtech on a school laptop is a conversation with the school, not with an app. What Brickify can do is make sure the phone isn't one more screen in the evenings, with homework apps allowed only during homework time.
  2. It doesn't scan images. That's Apple's and Google's job, and it's why I'm glad the government is pushing them. Brickify works alongside Screen Time and Family Link, so leave their nudity filters switched on.
  3. It keeps chatbots off the list. If a chatbot app isn't on the allowed list, it doesn't open. You decide whether one is ever added, and when.
  4. It's ready for the ban, whatever it covers. Fewer apps means fewer places for strangers, adverts and algorithms to reach your child, whichever services the final rules end up covering.
