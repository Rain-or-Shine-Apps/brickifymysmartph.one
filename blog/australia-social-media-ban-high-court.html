Australia's social media ban in court: what UK parents should know

The ruling is still to come, but the UK's own ban starts next spring. Here's what was argued, what the numbers show and what you can do now.

12 November 2026 · By Anne Mundy

← Back to Parenting & phones

Australia's under-16 social media ban had its big day in court last week. The High Court heard the challenge on 3 and 4 November and will rule later. The UK plans to use Australia's ban as its model, so the arguments and the early results are worth a few minutes of any parent's time.

What the court heard

Reddit and a campaign group, the Digital Freedom Project, say the ban is unconstitutional because it breaches an implied freedom of political communication in Australia's constitution. Reddit also wants the court to declare that it shouldn't be covered by the ban at all. Its barrister called the law the "worst possible outcome", because children lose safe content but can still use adults' accounts.

The Australian government told the court the ban is needed to protect children's brains. Its lawyers set out seven risks from the way platforms are designed: addictive use, poor sleep, depression, anxiety, suicidal thoughts, worsened disordered eating and intensified ADHD symptoms. They described the effect on political communication as "indirect in nature and modest in extent".

The court has reserved its decision and I haven't seen a date for it.

Is Australia's ban working?

It depends what you measure.

  • Accounts are down. Australia's online safety regulator, eSafety, says the share of under-16s holding accounts fell from 49.7% to 31.3%, with more than five million accounts restricted.
  • Use has barely moved. In a regulator study of children aged 10 to 15, more than 8 in 10 were still using social media about three months in, compared with nearly 86% before.
  • Many were never checked. About half of the children who kept their accounts said the platform never checked their age. The regulator's findings point to platforms failing to put effective checks in place.

Both can be true, partly because plenty of content can still be seen without an account. Fewer accounts doesn't automatically mean less time on the apps.

What it means for the UK

A ruling on Australia's constitution won't decide UK law, but the UK's plans are worth checking now. According to the government's fact sheet:

  • Timing: the changes are planned for spring 2027, and the Culture Secretary has said March.
  • Likely platforms: Instagram, YouTube, TikTok, Snapchat, Facebook and X are named from Australia's model, though that isn't presented as the final UK list.
  • Not included: messaging services such as WhatsApp and Signal, and dedicated educational platforms.
  • Gaming and other services: livestreaming and contact from strangers will be restricted for under-16s.
  • Age checks: Ofcom will set out the options for proving someone is over 16.

Four things you can do before spring

  1. Talk about it early. Ask which apps your child actually uses and what they'd miss. It's an easier conversation now than the week an account disappears.
  2. Check the age on their accounts. Some Australian children kept access because their account already said they were 16 or over.
  3. Look beyond the ban. WhatsApp isn't covered, and neither are group chats on it. Decide what you're happy with there, and in the games they play.
  4. Set up the phone itself. Rules on the platforms depend on the platforms. Screen Time and Family Link work on the device, whatever happens in court.
